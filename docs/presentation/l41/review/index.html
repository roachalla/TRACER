Gravity waves on water of finite depth

Learning objectives

By the end of this lecturette, you will be able to:

  • Explain how the bottom no-penetration condition modifies the potential for waves on a pool of finite depth
  • Derive the finite-depth dispersion relation from the determinant condition and write it using hyperbolic functions
  • Identify the deep-water limit as H→∞ and check that it recovers the earlier result
  • Apply the small-argument approximation tanh(x)≈x to show long waves are non-dispersive with speed gH

1Setting up boundary conditions for finite depth

This slide sets up waves on a pool of finite depth, which means gravity waves on water of depth H>0.

H>0
(1.1)

The wavelength λ is compared with the depth H. When λ≪H, we have the deep-water approximation.

λ≪H
(1.2)

When λ≫H, we have the shallow-water approximation.

λ≫H
(1.3)

The case in between is λ~H.

λ~H
(1.4)

The potential ϕ satisfies Laplace's equation.

∂2ϕ∂x2+∂2ϕ∂z2=0
(1.5)

At the free surface, z=0, there are two boundary conditions, shown in the black box on the slide.

(∂ϕ∂t)z=0+gη=0
(1.6)
∂η∂t−(∂ϕ∂z)z=0=0
(1.7)

At the bottom, z=−H, the no-penetration condition applies. This is the third boundary condition.

(∂ϕ∂z)z=−H=0
(1.8)

To get the dispersion relation, we substitute the expression for the solution into these boundary conditions. Then we set the coefficients of the stationary sine wave in both equations to zero and eliminate the unknowns. The unknowns are a, b, e and f, and in general they are complex.

λ→∞
(1.9)

Key takeaways

  • Finite depth H>0 is compared with wavelength λ: λ≪H is deep water, λ≫H is shallow water.
  • The potential obeys Laplace's equation, with two conditions at z=0 and no-penetration at z=−H.

2Applying the bottom condition to the potential

The slide continues the deep-water approximation by writing the velocity potential and the surface elevation as products of functions of x, z and t.

ϕ=[A′cos(kx)+B′sin(kx)][Cekz+De−kz]eiωt
(2.1)
η=[Ecos(kx)+Fsin(kx)]eiωt
(2.2)

Next, the bottom boundary condition is applied at z=−H, where the vertical derivative of the potential must vanish.

(∂ϕ∂z)z=−H=0
(2.3)

Differentiating the depth-dependent factor gives a condition on the two coefficients.

k[Ce−kH−De+kH]=0
(2.4)

This leads to a relation between C and D.

C=De2kH
(2.5)

Solving for D gives the boxed result.

D=Ce−2kH
(2.6)

Substituting it into the potential gives the following form.

ϕ=[A′cos(kx)+B′sin(kx)][Cekz+Ce−kz−2kH]eiωt
(2.7)

Now the constants are combined: A′C is renamed A, and B′C is renamed B.

A′C=A
(2.8)
B′C=B
(2.9)

The potential then takes its final form, with the exponential bracket replacing the bracket that contained C.

ϕ=[Acos(kx)+Bsin(kx)][ekz+e−kz−2kH]eiωt
(2.10)

The surface elevation is unchanged, so E stays the same.

η=[Ecos(kx)+Fsin(kx)]eiωt
(2.11)

You can substitute these expressions back into the equations to cross-check that they are correct.

Key takeaways

  • The bottom condition at z=−H requires the vertical derivative of the potential to vanish, giving a relation between C and D.
  • Renaming A′C as A and B′C as B gives the final potential; E is unchanged.

3Matrix system and the finite-depth quartic

As before, we have four equations in four unknowns, A, B, E and F.

iω(1+e−2kH)A+gE=0
(3.1)
iω(1+e−2kH)B+gF=0
(3.2)
iωE−kA(1−e−2kH)=0
(3.3)
iωF−kB(1−e−2kH)=0
(3.4)

We can write them in matrix form, as a homogeneous system.

[iω(1+e−2kH)0g00iω(1+e−2kH)0g−k(1−e−2kH)0iω00−k(1−e−2kH)0iω][ABEF]=[0000]
(3.5)

To check the validity of these equations, take the limit H→∞.

H→∞
(3.6)

All the exponential terms drop out, because they go to 0. In that limit you should recover exactly the equation we found earlier for surface gravity waves in deep water.

(ω2−gk)2=0
(3.7)

Try this on your own and compare it with the earlier result, to make sure the deep-water limit gives the same equation as before.

Next, set the determinant of the matrix to zero. This gives the dispersion relation, and once again you get a quartic.

||=0⇒{ω2(1+e−2kH)−gk(1−e−2kH)}2=0
(3.8)

It is written so that, as H→∞, the exponential terms go to 0 and you recover the quartic from the deep-water case. So this generalizes that result, and the extra factors represent the effect of finite depth.

Working this out gives an expression for ω2.

ω2=gk(1−e−2kH1+e−2kH)
(3.9)

We can simplify it by multiplying the numerator and denominator by an exponential, which turns the expression into exponentials of 2kH and −2kH. These are related to hyperbolic functions.

Key takeaways

  • The four equations in A, B, E, F form a homogeneous matrix system, and H→∞ should recover the deep-water equation.
  • Setting the determinant to zero gives a quartic that generalizes the deep-water one, with extra factors for finite depth.

4Rewriting the dispersion relation with hyperbolic functions

Writing the exponentials in hyperbolic form gives the dispersion relation for surface gravity waves on a pool of finite depth. Starting from the expression with exponentials, we have this relation.

ω2=gk(ekH−e−kHekH+e−kH)
(4.1)

To rewrite it, we use hyperbolic functions, which are defined by taking the exponential and splitting it into an even part and an odd part.

ex=ex+e−x2+ex−e−x2
(4.2)

The even part, ex+e−x2, is the hyperbolic cosine of x, and the odd part, ex−e−x2, is the hyperbolic sine of x.

ex=cosh(x)+sinh(x)
(4.3)

The ratio of the odd part to the even part is the hyperbolic tangent.

tanh(x)=ex−e−xex+e−x
(4.4)

The ratio of exponentials in the relation above is exactly this hyperbolic form, so we can write the dispersion relation compactly, with H the depth of the pool.

ω2=gktanh(kH)
(4.5)

Next, we consider the limit in which the wavelength becomes very large and the wavenumber goes to zero.

λ→∞
(4.6)
k→0
(4.7)

Earlier we found that the phase speed of gravity waves increases without bound in deep water.

Key takeaways

  • Even and odd parts of the exponential are the hyperbolic cosine and sine; their ratio is the hyperbolic tangent.
  • This gives a compact dispersion relation for a pool of depth H.

5Unbounded phase speed in capillary-gravity waves

The blue curve in the figure is the capillary-gravity dispersion relation, and it is given by the phase speed expression below.

cp=(gk+Tkρ)1/2
(5.1)

Notice that the blue curve increases without bound as k grows, so the phase speed becomes bigger and bigger.

k→0, cp~(gk)1/2
(5.2)
k→∞, cp~(Tkρ)1/2
(5.3)
gk=Tkρ⇒k~(ρgT)1/2
(5.4)
Blue curve: CG dispersion relation
(5.5)
cp=gk
(5.6)

Key takeaways

  • The capillary-gravity phase speed increases without bound as k grows.

6How finite depth corrects unbounded speed

Notice that the finite depth changes how the waves behave. The behavior described here gets rectified once we put in a finite depth. The further we go outward, the longer the wave is and the faster it travels.

Key takeaways

  • Putting in a finite depth rectifies the unbounded behavior.

7Short waves behave as in deep water

Earlier we saw that the effect of finite depth starts to show up when the wavelength of the wave becomes comparable to the depth of the pool. To see how this works, start with a pool of finite depth. Waves whose wavelengths are very short compared to the depth behave as if they are travelling on deep water.

Key takeaways

  • Waves much shorter than the depth behave as if on deep water.

8Waves feeling the bottom need correction

When the wavelength becomes comparable to the depth, the waves start to feel the bottom. At that point, a correction to the dispersion relation becomes necessary. This matters in particular if you take the limit of the wavelength λ going to infinity.

Key takeaways

  • When wavelength is comparable to depth, waves feel the bottom and the dispersion relation needs correction.

9Long-wave limit gives non-dispersive waves

Let's see what happens to the dispersion relation for long waves. We hold the depth H of the pool fixed and let the wavelength go to infinity.

H fixed, λ→∞
(9.1)

When λ→∞, the wavenumber goes to zero, so k→0 and therefore kH→0.

λ→∞, k→0
(9.2)
kH→0
(9.3)

To see what the dispersion relation does here, we need the behavior of tanh(x) for very small x. Recall that tanh(x) is defined through exponentials.

tanh(x)=ex−e−xex+e−x
(9.4)

For very small x, a Taylor series expansion shows that tanh(x) is just x.

tanh(x)~x
(9.5)

So for sufficiently long waves, tanh(kH) is approximately kH, and keeping only the first term gives the following approximation.

ω2~gk(kH)=k2gH
(9.6)

Notice what has happened. For sufficiently long waves, the phase speed is ω/k, which is just gH.

cp=ωk=gH
(9.7)

This is unlike the other examples we have seen so far, where the phase speed was a function of k.

Once the waves are long enough, they behave as pure gravity waves on a pool much shallower than their wavelength. They are long waves, and they are non-dispersive: their phase speed does not depend on wavelength.

cp(k)→Non-disp
(9.8)

This has many consequences for how these waves propagate, especially when many of them travel together. We will see some of this later.

Key takeaways

  • For kH→0, tanh(kH)≈kH, so the phase speed is gH.
  • Long waves are non-dispersive: phase speed does not depend on wavelength.

Study questions

  1. What three boundary conditions are used for finite-depth waves, and where do they apply?

    Show answer

    Two conditions apply at the free surface z=0, and the no-penetration condition applies at the bottom z=−H.

  2. How is the dispersion relation obtained from the matrix system?

    Show answer

    Set the determinant of the matrix of the homogeneous system to zero, which gives a quartic.

  3. How can you check the finite-depth equations against the deep-water case?

    Show answer

    Take H→∞. The exponential terms go to 0, and you should recover the earlier deep-water equation.

  4. How are the hyperbolic cosine, sine and tangent defined from the exponential?

    Show answer

    The even part ex+e−x2 is the hyperbolic cosine, the odd part ex−e−x2 is the hyperbolic sine, and their ratio odd over even is the hyperbolic tangent.

  5. Why does the phase speed become gH for long waves, and what does this imply?

    Show answer

    As λ→∞, kH→0 and tanh(kH)≈kH, so ω/k is gH. This does not depend on wavelength, so the waves are non-dispersive.

  6. When does finite depth start to matter?

    Show answer

    When the wavelength becomes comparable to the depth, so the waves feel the bottom. Much shorter waves behave as in deep water.

Glossary

Deep-water approximation
The case λ≪H, where the wavelength is much smaller than the depth.
Dispersion relation
The relation obtained by setting the determinant of the matrix to zero, giving a quartic and then an expression for ω2.
Hyperbolic tangent
The ratio of the odd part to the even part of the exponential, i.e. hyperbolic sine over hyperbolic cosine.
No-penetration condition
The bottom boundary condition at z=−H that the vertical derivative of the potential vanishes.
Non-dispersive waves
Waves whose phase speed does not depend on wavelength.
Shallow-water approximation
The case λ≫H, where the wavelength is much larger than the depth.
